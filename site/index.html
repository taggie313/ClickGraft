Why HP Click is slow

In 2020 Apple started putting its own processors in Macs instead of Intel's. They're called Apple Silicon: the M-series chips, M1 through M5 and whatever comes next. Older Intel apps still run, because macOS translates them while they go, using a system called Rosetta. HP Click is one of those apps. The translation is why it takes a minute to open and why your clicks land a beat late.

Here's the odd part. HP already builds most of HP Click for Apple Silicon. The page layout, the colour handling, the print engine: all of it compiled for your Mac's processor, all of it sitting inside the app you have installed right now. What HP packages them with is an Intel engine (Electron, the framework HP Click is built on).

ClickGraft swaps that engine for the Apple Silicon build of the same Electron version, which HP downloads rather than writes. The rest of the app is left as HP shipped it.

What it does to your Mac

  • Your HP Click isn't modified. It's opened for reading and left alone.
  • You end up with two apps. Your original, and HP Click (Apple Silicon) next to it. Your printers and settings carry over.
  • To undo it, drag the new app to the Trash. There's no uninstaller because there's nothing else to remove.
  • No HP software is distributed. ClickGraft contains none of HP's code. It works on the copy of HP Click you installed yourself.

Before anything gets written, the app shows you the plan: what it reads, what it creates, every change it makes and why, and every file it downloads along with the fingerprint it checks that file against. Nothing happens until you press the button.

What you need

  • A Mac with Apple Silicon. Any M-series processor. On an Intel Mac there's nothing to fix.
  • HP Click 4.10.42, 4.8.118 or 4.8.117, already installed. ClickGraft ships no HP software and can't get it for you.
  • Apple's Command Line Tools. Free, from Apple. Most Macs used for design or print work have them already. If yours doesn't, macOS offers to install them the first time you open ClickGraft: accept it, wait for the download to finish, then open ClickGraft again.

Installing it

  1. Download ClickGraft.zip and unzip it.
  2. Drag ClickGraft.app wherever you like. Applications is fine.
  3. Double-click it and follow the six screens.

It's signed with an Apple Developer ID and notarized, so there's no right-clicking to open it and no warning about an unidentified developer. macOS asks once whether you meant to open something you downloaded, and the first launch takes a few extra seconds while it checks with Apple. Both happen once and then stop.

Don't run both apps at once

The two apps share your printers and settings. That sharing has a side effect: open one while the other is running and the second quits on the spot, with no window and no error. It looks like it failed to start. Quit one before you open the other.

Checking what you downloaded

The strongest check doesn't involve us at all. The app is signed with an Apple Developer ID and notarized, and macOS verifies that against Apple's own certificates before it will run. In Terminal:

spctl -a -vvv -t install /path/to/ClickGraft.app

It should answer accepted, source=Notarized Developer ID, and name the developer. If it says anything else, don't run it. That check is worth more than any fingerprint printed here, because it's verified against Apple rather than against this page.

Which versions work

HP Click 4.10.42, 4.8.118 and 4.8.117. ClickGraft won't touch a version it hasn't been tested against. It needs to know exactly where its changes go, and a wrong guess damages your app rather than the tool. All three have been built and printed from, not merely inspected.

If HP's download page offers you 4.8.117, that isn't your mistake. HP published 4.10.42 for macOS on 26 August 2026 without listing it: the page still advertises the June build, and the newer one is reachable only if you already know its address. ClickGraft handles either.

4.8.118 was never a download at all. It only ever arrived as a background auto-update, which is why people who have it can't reinstall it and, until now, couldn't patch it. If that's you, ClickGraft works on it.

New versions shouldn't stay unsupported for long. When HP ships an update, most of the work is mechanical: read the new app, check that every change still lands where it should, confirm the engine version matches. The intent is to have a ClickGraft release out not long after HP's, and that turnaround is a priority rather than an afterthought.

If you've already updated and ClickGraft doesn't recognise what you have, the app will write a description of your version for you. Open an issue with that attached and it gets sorted faster.

Is this allowed?

ClickGraft distributes no HP code, and it changes nothing about what HP Click is allowed to do. Licensing, supplies, features, any restriction HP put in the software: all untouched. It swaps one downloaded component so the Apple Silicon code HP already wrote can run natively, and it repairs a bug in HP's own files that throws an error every time the app starts, on Intel Macs too.

Keeping the scope that narrow is the point, and contributions are held to the same line.

Getting in touch

The quickest way is from inside ClickGraft. When it finishes it offers Share how it went and Report a problem, and both carry what someone would otherwise have to ask you for: the versions involved, which checks passed, and the build log. You can read every word before it's sent, and nothing goes anywhere unless you press the button.

Otherwise: clickgraft@elusive.net, or open an issue on GitHub if you'd rather it were public and searchable — which is often more useful, because the next person with the same problem can find it.

This is one person working on it, so a reply may take a few days. Telling me it worked is genuinely worth doing: passing every check is not the same as a page coming out of a plotter, and the only way anyone learns it holds up on a printer I've never touched is if someone says so.

What this site records. No cookies, no scripts, no analytics service, nothing shared with anyone. The server keeps an ordinary access log for one reason: to see whether this is worth maintaining. Your address is cut short before it's written down — the last part is thrown away — so it counts visitors without identifying them. What's left is kept, because it's a few lines a day and there's nothing in it that points at a person.
