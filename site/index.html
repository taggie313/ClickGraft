What ClickGraft used to do, and what this page is now

Until September 2026 there was one problem and one answer. HP Click for Mac was an Intel application. On an Apple Silicon Mac it ran through Rosetta and it crawled — nearly a minute to finish starting, and it froze if you touched it while it worked. ClickGraft existed to fix exactly that: it made a copy of your HP Click and put the Apple Silicon engine HP already builds into the copy. That was the whole project, and the numbers below are what it bought.

Then HP shipped 4.11.31, and most of that job ended. On 17 September 2026 HP published an HP Click built for Apple Silicon itself. If it supports your printer, it is a better answer than anything this project can make, and the honest thing to say is: install it and go. This page says so at the top, and the app says so too when it finds one.

What did not end is everyone HP left behind. HP removed five DesignJet models from HP Click's supported list in 4.8.118 and has not put them back. It moved HP Click's minimum macOS from 10.10 to 12.0 somewhere in the 4.7 releases, so anyone on an older Mac is offered a build that cannot start. It published one version only as an auto-update, removed another from its download page, and left a third as a broken file on its own server. None of those are graft problems, and all of them are somebody's actual Monday morning.

So this page changed shape. It is still where ClickGraft is published, and the graft still does what it always did for the people it applies to. But most of what follows is now the other thing: which version you should run, where to get it when HP won't serve it, what HP changed and when, and which of it can be fixed and which cannot. All of it is measured from HP's own builds rather than repeated from anywhere, and where something has only been read and not tested, it says so.

Why HP Click is slow

In 2020 Apple started putting its own processors in Macs instead of Intel's. They're called Apple Silicon: the M-series chips, M1 through M5 and whatever comes next. Older Intel apps still run, because macOS translates them while they go, using a system called Rosetta. HP Click is one of those apps. The translation is why it takes a minute to open and why your clicks land a beat late.

On macOS 27 it's no longer only about speed. Upgrading to macOS 27 Golden Gate removes Rosetta, so HP Click as HP ships it won't open until Rosetta is installed again. macOS asks the first time you open it, and the install is quick. The bigger change is next year: Apple has said macOS 27 is the last version where Rosetta runs apps like HP Click, so the Intel versions of HP Click won't open on macOS 28. HP has since released one that will: 4.11.31 is native. The Intel versions matter because 4.8.117 is still the only one that supports five older DesignJets, and a ClickGraft copy of it doesn't use Rosetta at all. On 14 September 2026, the day macOS 27 shipped, ClickGraft was run on a Mac with Rosetta removed: it made its copy, and that copy opened normally.

Here's the odd part. HP already builds most of HP Click for Apple Silicon. The page layout, the colour handling, the print engine: all of it compiled for your Mac's processor, all of it sitting inside the app you have installed right now. What HP packages them with is an Intel engine (Electron, the framework HP Click is built on).

ClickGraft swaps that engine for the Apple Silicon build of the same Electron version, which HP downloads rather than writes. The rest of the app is left as HP shipped it.

What it does to your Mac

  • Your HP Click isn't modified. It's opened for reading and left alone.
  • You end up with two apps. Your original, and HP Click (Apple Silicon) next to it. Your printers and settings carry over.
  • To undo it, drag the new app to the Trash. There's no uninstaller. If ClickGraft had to fetch a small program to do its work, that stays in ~/Library/Application Support/ClickGraft — about 50 MB — and you can delete that folder too.
  • The copy never asks HP for updates. Your original asks every time it starts, and downloads the whole application — around 570 MB — if HP's feed names something newer. A copy of 4.8.117 stays 4.8.117 on its own, so if you print to one of the five printers HP dropped, “don't update” isn't something you have to keep remembering.
  • No HP software is distributed. ClickGraft contains none of HP's code. It works on the copy of HP Click you installed yourself.

Before anything gets written, the app shows you the plan: what it reads, what it creates, every change it makes and why, and every file it downloads along with the fingerprint it checks that file against. Nothing happens until you press the button.

What you need

  • A Mac with Apple Silicon. Any M-series processor: that's the Mac the copy is for. An Intel Mac can make a copy for an Apple Silicon Mac — tick the box ClickGraft offers — but it can't test-launch it, so open the copy on the Mac it was made for.
  • macOS 15 or later. Files HP ships inside HP Click, and the support files ClickGraft adds, are built for macOS 15 and later, so the copy needs it too. On an older Mac, ClickGraft says so before it downloads anything. On macOS 12, 13 or 14, install HP's own Apple Silicon version instead: HP lists 4.11.31 for macOS 12 to 26. If you print to one of the five printers below, 4.11.31 doesn't have them either, so today there is nothing to move to: keep the 4.8.117 you have, which goes on working there as it does now, and come back if that Mac moves to macOS 15.
  • HP Click 4.10.42, 4.8.118 or 4.8.117, already installed. ClickGraft ships no HP software and can't get it for you.
  • Nothing else from Apple. ClickGraft used to need Apple's Command Line Tools: a download of several gigabytes, behind an administrator password you may not have. It doesn't any more. On a Mac that already has them it uses those; on a Mac that doesn't, it offers to fetch one small program of its own — 17 MB, once, into your own Library folder, with nothing installed into macOS and nobody asked for a password.

Installing it

  1. Download {{ZIP_NAME}} and unzip it.
  2. Drag ClickGraft.app wherever you like. Applications is fine.
  3. Double-click it and follow the six screens.

It's signed with an Apple Developer ID and notarized, so there's no right-clicking to open it and no warning about an unidentified developer. macOS asks once whether you meant to open something you downloaded, and the first launch takes a few extra seconds while it checks with Apple. Both happen once and then stop.

If you already have a copy, make it again

A copy carries the fixes of the ClickGraft that made it, and two of them are reason enough to make a new one: copies made before 1.5.8 still tried to send HP's crash reports unencrypted, and copies made before 1.5.9 can fail to open on macOS 15.0 to 15.3. Open ClickGraft {{VERSION}} and make the copy again. Since 1.5.9 the copy you have is kept until the new one has passed every check, and put back if it hasn't.

Don't run both apps at once

The two apps share your printers and settings. That sharing has a side effect: open one while the other is running and the second quits on the spot, with no window and no error. It looks like it failed to start. Quit one before you open the other.

Checking what you downloaded

The strongest check doesn't involve us at all. The app is signed with an Apple Developer ID and notarized, and macOS verifies that against Apple's own certificates before it will run. In Terminal:

spctl -a -vvv -t install /path/to/ClickGraft.app

It should answer accepted, source=Notarized Developer ID, and name the developer. If it says anything else, don't run it. That check is worth more than any fingerprint printed here, because it's verified against Apple rather than against this page.

The same fingerprint is published on the GitHub release, which is a genuinely independent place to compare it against.

If your Mac has no developer tools, ClickGraft offers to fetch one small program it needs — about 17 MB, once. You can check that the same way. The file it fetches, and the fingerprint it checks it against before installing anything, are both published here: python-pin.json names the version and the SHA-256, and the file itself is ClickGraft-python-<version>.zip beside it. ClickGraft does this check itself and installs nothing that fails it; the files are published so you don't have to take that on trust.

Which versions work

To see which one you have, open your Applications folder, click HP Click once and press ⌘I. The Version line is the number the rest of this page is about. ClickGraft reads that same line, and lists it for every HP Click it finds, including the ones it can't work with.

HP Click 4.11.31 doesn't need ClickGraft. HP published it on 17 September 2026, and it is built for Apple Silicon: every part of it runs natively, with no Rosetta. If it supports your printer, moving to it is the better fix, and ClickGraft recognises it and says so. It has the same printer list as 4.10.42, so the five printers below are still missing.

You have to download 4.11.31 yourself. HP Click won't update itself to it: on 19 September 2026, HP's Mac update feed still named 4.8.118. Use the installer link in the table below, or HP's download page, which lists 4.11.31 for macOS 12 to 26. macOS 27 isn't in HP's list yet; choose 26, because 4.11.31 runs on 27.

HP Click 4.10.42, 4.8.118 and 4.8.117. ClickGraft won't touch a version it hasn't been tested against. It needs to know exactly where its changes go, and a wrong guess damages your app rather than the tool. All three have been built and printed from, not merely inspected.

4.10.42 was never on HP's download page, and its installer is gone. HP published it for macOS on 26 August 2026 without listing it, and has since removed the .dmg from its server. The app itself is still there as a .zip, in the table below. HP's page now offers 4.11.31 for macOS 12 to 26, and 4.8.117 if you choose macOS 11 or earlier, although 4.8.117 needs macOS 12 as well.

4.8.118 was never published as a download. It only ever arrived as a background auto-update, which is why people who have it can't reinstall it from HP's page. ClickGraft works on it — and, it turns out, you can get it after all. See below.

Getting a particular version from HP

HP keeps its Mac builds in two separate places, and the one people know about is the smaller of them. The download page serves .dmg installers. The auto-updater pulls from a different directory entirely, as zipped copies of the app — and that directory holds builds that were never published as a DMG at all. Both are HP's own servers; these are the addresses HP Click itself uses.

HP keeps only the newest installer. On 9 October 2026 every .dmg except the current one had been deleted — 4.11.31's included, a week after it was the version HP was shipping. The zip directory behaves the other way: builds from June are still there. So a .dmg link below is good only until HP's next release, while the .zip links should keep working. A zip is the app itself: unzip it and drag it to Applications, there is no installer to run.

4.8.118 drops five printers. Check yours first.

HP removed these models from HP Click's supported-printer list. They work in 4.8.117 and in nothing since:

DesignJet T310 · T320 · T350 · T720 · T750

The list went from 117 entries to 110 in 4.8.118 — HP names each carriage width separately, so those seven entries are these five plotters — and 4.10.38, 4.10.42 and 4.11.31 carry the same shortened list. So the version to stay on is 4.8.117, not 4.8.118 — an earlier version of this page said either would do, which was wrong.

If you own one of these, install 4.8.117 and graft that. Do not update, and if you already have, 4.8.117 is still on HP's server using the links above.

ClickGraft cannot fix this one, and we tried. Putting those seven entries back is a one-line change to a file inside the app, and the result is an app that will not open at all: HP Click checks that list against its own signature at startup and refuses to run when it doesn't match, with “Cannot verify the integrity of HP Click… The application will now close.” That was tested directly — two builds identical but for that one file, the modified one dead on launch and the stock one printing normally. This is a decision HP made about which printers it still supports, not something the graft can undo.

If your Mac can't run macOS 12

HP's download page will hand you a build that cannot start. Choose macOS 11 or earlier there and it offers 4.8.117, and 4.8.117 needs macOS 12: it will not launch on macOS 11 or anything older, whatever the page implies. Nor does HP offer anything else — its list of previous versions is empty for every macOS it names. That is a dead end, and it is HP's page that creates it.

HP does still serve builds that run on an older Mac, in the same updater directory as the table above and linked from no HP page at all. Ten of them were checked on 26 September 2026 and all ten answered: 3.7.83, 3.7.85, 4.0.57, 4.0.59, 4.1.178, 4.3.27, 4.3.29, 4.4.59, 4.5.21 and 4.6.47. Every one declares macOS 10.10. The newest is 4.6.47, published 29 January 2026, and it is the one to use — the others are older with no advantage.

The five printers have no answer on an older Mac

If you print to a DesignJet T310, T320, T350, T720 or T750 and your Mac cannot run macOS 12, there is no version of HP Click for you. Not an awkward one — none.

Those five are listed in 4.8.117 and in no other release. HP added them in 4.8.117 and removed them again in 4.8.118, and every older build carries 109 entries without them — 4.6.47, 4.5.21, 4.4.59 and 3.7.83 were each opened and their lists counted. So the only release that drives those plotters is the one that needs macOS 12, and the only builds that run on your Mac are the ones that never listed them.

ClickGraft cannot bridge it either. It needs macOS 15 itself, so it will not run on your Mac — and it would not help if it did: the graft exists to make an Intel app run properly on an Apple Silicon Mac, and a Mac too old for macOS 12 is an Intel Mac, where HP Click already runs natively. The problem the graft solves is not the one you have.

What is left is a newer Mac, or printing without HP Click — macOS can drive these plotters over AirPrint or IPP directly, which loses HP Click's nesting and roll optimisation but does print.

One thing you do not have to worry about: being updated off a build that works. HP's Mac updater has never been able to install anything — its installer helper fails on a framework missing from the app, and an install log from one Mac held 1,366 consecutive failures. HP's feed will keep naming 4.8.118 and your copy will keep ignoring it.

Going the other way, 4.10.42 is the better build if your printer is not on that list: it groups the page list by source file, lets you drag pages between documents, and its window shrinks to 960×550 instead of demanding 1291×720, which matters on a laptop. It also stops showing an unloaded Sheet source by default — there is a new checkbox in Preferences to put that back if you miss it.

If you have 4.10.38, the project would like a copy

HP did publish 4.10.38 to that second directory, but the file there is broken — seven megabytes of a build that should be five hundred and seventy, an upload that stopped early and was never fixed. It cannot be unzipped, so it is not linked above and nobody can obtain it from HP any more.

If 4.10.38 is what is installed on your Mac, a copy of it would let ClickGraft support that version for everyone else still on it. Nothing automated is needed — get in touch and we will sort out the details. The same goes for any version not listed here: if ClickGraft doesn't recognise what you have, that is the missing piece, and your copy is the only way to close the gap.

New versions shouldn't stay unsupported for long. When HP ships an update, most of the work is mechanical: read the new app, check that every change still lands where it should, confirm the engine version matches. The intent is to have a ClickGraft release out not long after HP's, and that turnaround is a priority rather than an afterthought.

If you've already updated and ClickGraft doesn't recognise what you have, the app will write a description of your version for you. Open an issue with that attached and it gets sorted faster.

What this project can and can't fix

The useful half of a clearinghouse is knowing which problems have an answer. These do not, and each was established rather than assumed.

  • Putting the five printers back into a newer HP Click. It is a one-line change to a file inside the app, and the result will not open at all: HP Click checks that list against its own signature at startup. Tested directly, on two builds identical but for that one file. Details.
  • Making HP Click support a printer HP does not list. Which printers HP Click accepts is a decision HP made and recorded inside the app, behind the same signature check. It is not a statement about your hardware — the five printers above work perfectly well and simply stopped being listed — but it is not something a patch can change.
  • Making an old HP Click run on Apple Silicon. Everything before 4.8.117 is built on Electron 8, which has no Apple Silicon build at all, and HP's own printing add-ons in those versions are Intel-only. A newer engine cannot be dropped in either: those add-ons are compiled against the exact runtime they shipped with, and HP does not publish their source. ClickGraft works by switching on arm64 code HP already built, and in those versions there is none.
  • Helping anyone below macOS 15 with the tool itself. ClickGraft needs macOS 15 to run. If your Mac is older than that, the answer is a version of HP Click rather than a copy of one, which is what the table above and the older-Mac section are for.

And one honest gap: no HP Click below 12.0 has been opened on a Mac old enough to need it, because nobody working on this has one. Those rows rest on what the builds declare and on reading their libraries, not on a test. Tell us if you try it and that will stop being true.

Is this allowed?

ClickGraft distributes no HP code, and it changes nothing about what HP Click is allowed to do. Licensing, supplies, features, any restriction HP put in the software: all untouched. It swaps one downloaded component so the Apple Silicon code HP already wrote can run natively, and on 4.8.117 and 4.8.118 it repairs a bug in HP's own files that throws an error every time the app starts, on Intel Macs too. 4.10.42 stopped loading the file with that bug, so there is nothing to repair there and ClickGraft leaves it alone.

Keeping the scope that narrow is the point, and contributions are held to the same line.

If you print other people's files

A job turns up built with fonts you don't have. macOS doesn't stop to tell you — it quietly substitutes something else, prints it, and you find out when the client does. untofu is another tool I wrote: it answers those requests instead, finding the font, fetching it, and handing it to the application that asked. Getting in touch

The quickest way is from inside ClickGraft. When it finishes it offers Share how it went and Report a problem, and both carry what someone would otherwise have to ask you for: the versions involved, which checks passed, and the build log. You can read every word before it's sent, and nothing goes anywhere unless you press the button.

If a build fails, that report also has a blank for an email address. It is optional and it starts empty — a report without one is still worth sending, and most are. It exists because the alternative is a dead end in one direction: the first failure ClickGraft ever received said exactly what was wrong, it was fixed the same day, and there was no way at all to tell the person who reported it. If you do fill it in, it will be used to reply about that report — to ask a question, or to say when it's fixed — and for nothing else. No mailing list, no newsletter, not passed to anyone. It is the only thing in a report that identifies you, and it is there only if you type it.

Otherwise: clickgraft@elusive.net, or open an issue on GitHub if you'd rather it were public and searchable — which is often more useful, because the next person with the same problem can find it.

This is one person working on it, so a reply may take a few days. Telling me it worked is genuinely worth doing: passing every check is not the same as a page coming out of a plotter, and the only way anyone learns it holds up on a printer I've never touched is if someone says so.

What this site records. No cookies, no scripts, no analytics service, nothing shared with anyone. The server keeps an ordinary access log for one reason: to see whether this is worth maintaining. Your address is cut short before it's written down — the last part is thrown away — so it counts visitors without identifying them. What's left is kept, because it's a few lines a day and there's nothing in it that points at a person.

What a report contains. No report is sent unless you press the button, and the exact text is on screen before you do. What arrives here is that text, plus a two-letter country code Cloudflare adds — never your address. Paths through your home folder are stripped on your Mac and stripped again when the report lands, so your account name doesn't travel with it. The email blank is optional, starts empty, and is used only to reply about that one report.

How long it's kept. Reports are deleted 90 days after they arrive. What survives is a count — how many, of which kind, from which country — because that is the number that says whether the tool works, and it points at nobody. If you'd like yours removed sooner, or want to know what was in it, write to clickgraft@elusive.net and say roughly when you sent it.

The one thing the app does on its own. When ClickGraft opens, it asks this server whether a newer version exists — that's how it can tell you about a fix. That request lands in the same access log as any other visit: your shortened address, and the line your Mac sends with every request, which here names ClickGraft's version and roughly which macOS you're on. Nothing else. There's no account, no identifier, and nothing that ties one launch to another. It is the only thing ClickGraft sends without you pressing something.
